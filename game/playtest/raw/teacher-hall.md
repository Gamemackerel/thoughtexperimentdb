# teacher · the hall

## Summary
- The hall's five vignettes set their thought experiments up beautifully in plain words (Hart's "someone should have come by now", Lewis's "you could have stopped him, in a sense", the free-rider neighbours). The journal questions are classroom-ready. As a *teaching* set, though, they mostly let the player act on a **side question** rather than the experiment's own fork: whether to broadcast (Fermi), whether to switch off (Simulation), whether to wait (Monkeys).
- One vignette actively teaches a misconception. Infinite Monkeys scripts "a line of Hamlet after three million years... bound to happen", which is off by ~60 orders of magnitude and contradicts its own notebook.
- Decision timing is broken in the Grandfather Paradox. The question "what happens if you stop him?" is voiced after the gate option has expired, and ~10 s before the ending. A careful student who listens first is labelled "You let it be".
- Tragedy of the Commons has no visible private incentive, and the neighbours only overgraze because *you* did. The dilemma collapses into "don't press the bad button", and the game implies the tragedy needs a first bad actor.
- Each vignette covers only one position in its literature. The rival views the notebooks already name (branching time travel; privatisation vs. regulation vs. Ostrom; METI) aren't playable. None of the five has the replay twist or "the narrator notices you chose differently" beat that GAME.md's template promises, so a second run teaches nothing new.
- Strong keeps: the simulation pull-back reveal, the commons' souring neighbours, the Fermi moon line, and every journal question.

## Findings
### hall
- **[minor] [hub/learning flow]** "Back to the house" on every hall vignette's game-over card drops you at the first-room spawn (0, 5.5), not beside the portal you used in the hall. In class, students go from one hall experiment to the next, so every return costs a ladder climb and a walk down the hall. GAME.md says players should "come back beside the portal they used".
  Evidence: after Grandfather, Simulation, Fermi and Commons, `level=house you at (0.0, 5.5)`.
  Suggestion: make the card's home button return to `ctx.from`'s room (the hall) and spawn at that portal. Label it "Back to the hall" there.
- **[idea] [learning]** The journal (the one place a student's reasoning is kept) is only in the first room. After finishing a hall vignette there's nowhere nearby to reread your answer or the to-do list.
  Suggestion: a second copy of the journal on a side table in the hall (the Magritte joke writes itself: "Ceci n'est pas un journal").
- **[minor] [journal]** The journal keeps only the *latest* ending per vignette. In Grandfather I reached "It had already happened" (tried 3 ways), then "You left the past alone". The journal now shows only the latter, next to an answer I wrote after the first run. For reflection, the history of choices is the valuable part ("first you fought it, then you left").
  Suggestion: store every ending reached, with its date (e.g. "It had already happened · You left the past alone"), and show which ending each answer was written after.
- **[polish] [text]** The apple gentleman's speech bubbles (four lines) stay stacked on screen while you walk the whole hall and into the clock portal (they were still listed on screen at t=36.9 s, 20 m away).
  Suggestion: fade `talk()` bubbles when the player leaves the radius.
- **[keep]** Portal labels with the experiment names appear as you walk past, and they get a ✓ once done. The ✓ is a clear progress map for a class.

### grandfather-paradox
- **[major] [decision design]** The invitation to act arrives after most of the choices have expired. The narrator asks "So what happens if you stop him?" at the moment grandfather reaches segment 3, and that is exactly when "Close the gate" gets disabled (`enabled: S.seg < 3`). The meeting happens about 10 s later, and you are 15+ m from the signpost. On run 1 I listened to the setup the way a careful student would, then did `use close the gate`: it was "disabled right now", he met her, and the card said **"You let it be"**. The game labelled me a passive observer when I had tried to act. The design says "time slows to a crawl at the choice", but here time never slows and the choice is gone before the question is asked.
  Evidence: run 1, question caption t=52.1 s, meeting (phase over) t=62.5 s; shot 008-t75.jpg. Code: `grandfather-paradox.js` l.98 and l.159.
  Suggestion: voice the question when you arrive (or make grandfather wait at the machine until the question has been voiced), and slow his walk right after it (the other vignettes' "slow" phase). Also keep the gate usable until he is actually through it (seg ≤ 2 && u < 0.3).
- **[major] [philosophy]** Every branch teaches the same position: self-consistency, with the Lewis "you can but you won't" reading in the ending text. That is a real and good position, but the grandfather paradox is a classroom favourite *because* it is a fork in the literature. The main rival, branching or many-worlds time travel (you do stop him, and you return to a present where you were never born or a timeline that isn't yours), doesn't exist as an option or even as a line. There's also no twist after the first run (GAME.md template, beat 5) and no "most drastic option" ending. Students will come away thinking physics has settled it.
  Suggestion: on replay, add a twist that lets one attempt succeed (e.g. the second time the gate holds). He doesn't arrive, the sepia scene splits into a second, slightly different-coloured copy of the town, and "Go home" takes you to a time machine that's no longer yours. Reflection: "You changed it. But whose past was it?" That turns the vignette into a comparison between two real positions, and the notebook can then name Novikov-style consistency vs. branching (Deutsch), neither of which it mentions now.
- **[minor] [feedback]** The consequence lines lag their causes by up to 10 s because they queue behind the scripted lines. I told him who I was at t=155.4; "He laughs... And he walks on." played at t=165.6, after the grandmother and question lines. The signpost failure played after that. For learning, cause and effect need to be next to each other.
  Suggestion: let the `*_fail` lines interrupt or jump ahead of the descriptive lines (or delay `see_gm`/`ask` while a fail line is pending).
- **[minor] [bug/narrative]** "Go home" right at the start: the intro line "That's your grandfather. Young, and in a hurry." plays *between* the two reflection lines ("You came all this way, and changed nothing." / "...grandfather..." / "Maybe that's the only story that works."). The intro coroutine isn't cancelled when the run ends.
  Evidence: t=274.5–282.1 s, run 5.
  Suggestion: check `S.phase === 'walk'` before `voice.say('see_gf')` in the intro async.
- **[polish] [text]** One attempt gives the card "You tried once. Each time, something ordinary got in the way." ("Each time" after "once"). Also "You came all this way and changed nothing" on "Go home" 2 s after arriving reads oddly: you didn't come any way at all.
  Suggestion: "You tried once. Something ordinary got in the way." For leaving early, something like "You left before anything could happen. Or before anything could fail to."
- **[minor] [decision design]** Standing in his path only counts if you're there before he gets close. On run 3 I tap-walked to (7, 0.9) and arrived just as he reached me; he walked past and the card said "You let it be" / "changed nothing". On run 4, standing there early, it counted ("He steps around you, and apologises.").
  Suggestion: count a block whenever the player is within ~1.6 m ahead of him, even for a moment, or make his step-around visibly deliberate so the player knows they tried.
- **[idea] [learning]** The four attempts (gate, sign, block, tell) are nicely ordinary, but the reflection is the same whichever ones you try. The one that matters philosophically, "Tell him who you are", is where a student would expect the past to be *causally loaded* (the bootstrap problem: maybe telling him is why they married). One line there would open a second classic puzzle for free.
  Suggestion: on "tell", have grandma overhear and smile, and on the card: "Maybe that's why they got on so well." It's a gentle hint at causal loops, with the notebook explaining it.
- **[keep]** The journal question ("could you change anything at all? What would stop you?") is excellent for class. It's open, it's about reasons, and it works whichever ending you got. The Lewis line "You could have stopped him, in a sense. You just didn't." is the best one-sentence summary of a hard paper I've seen.

### infinite-monkey
- **[major] [philosophy/maths]** The vignette teaches the opposite of the theorem's lesson. The progression is scripted: after 1 million years you get "the", after 2 million "to be", and after 3 million the whole line (`TARGETS` in `infinite-monkey.js`). The card then says "Three million years of noise, and then a line of Hamlet... It was simply bound to happen." With the page alphabet (≈37 symbols), "to be, or not to be, that is the question" is 41 characters, so roughly 37^41 ≈ 10^64 tries. Twelve monkeys typing a key a second would need vastly longer than the age of the universe. Even "to be" (5 chars, ~7×10^7 tries) takes about a year, not a million. Your own notebook says Borel used the monkeys for events "so improbable they will never be seen in practice." A student who plays the "found" ending leaves believing a few million years is enough. That is exactly the misconception teachers use this example to correct.
  Evidence: shot 029-found.jpg, card text at t=371.3 s; `TARGETS` l.16, card text l.105.
  Suggestion: keep the three pulls, but make the year counter honest and let it be the punchline. First pull: +1,000,000 years, the page has a real short word by chance (short words should actually show up, even on the 0-year page). Second pull: the lever says "Wait until the stars go out" and the counter jumps to 10^14 years; the page has *two* short words. Third: the counter runs off the plaque (10^60... in scientific notation), then the line. Card: "Longer than the universe will last, and then a line of Hamlet." That keeps "given forever, it happens" true and makes "forever is longer than it sounds" something you *feel*.
- **[minor] [decision design]** The only decision is "keep waiting" vs "walk out", and neither maps onto a position. The theorem has no sides, but the journal question does: it's about meaning without intention (the famous version is the poem written by waves in the sand, Knapp & Michaels' "Against Theory", 1982). Nothing in play sets that question up before the card asks it.
  Suggestion: after the line is found, give the player one act: "Keep the page" or "Put it back in the pile". The reflection names the tension ("You kept it. Something about it mattered, though nobody meant it."). Add Knapp & Michaels (or the wave-poem idea) to the notebook and journal to-dos so the question has a reading behind it.
- **[minor] [text]** The walk-out card still asks "The monkeys typed 'To be, or not to be' without meaning it. Do the words mean anything...?" You never saw them type it on that path.
  Suggestion: on the walk-out ending, make the question conditional: "If they ever did type it, would the words mean anything...?"
- **[polish] [text]** The narrator says "To be, or not to be" but the page marks "to be, or not to be, that is the question" (a longer target). The counter also stops at 1,010,417 / 3,031,250 years rather than a round million each time, which looks like an error to a student.
  Suggestion: clamp `years` to exact multiples of 1,000,000 when a wait ends.
- **[polish] [UI]** The last page stays visible behind the game-over card, and its text shows through the card's text and makes the question harder to read (shot 030-over.jpg).
  Suggestion: close `ctx.page` before `ctx.gameOver`.
- **[keep]** The lectern → lever → lectern loop is a clean, tactile way to *do* waiting. The highlighted word in noise (shot 028-page2.jpg) is a lovely image. "Forever takes a lot of bananas" and the all-q page are good jokes that don't break the scene.

### simulation-argument
- **[major] [philosophy/decision design]** The choice the player gets (switch the worlds off / leave them running) isn't the question the argument asks, and the game never connects the two. The argument is about *credence*: one of three things is true, and if sims are cheap you're probably in one. The narration does ask it ("how many are real? And which kind is yours?"), but no action expresses an answer. The actual choice is an *ethical* one about beings "to whom it's all real". The reflection then treats it only prudentially: switching off gets "If your world is one of those, somebody could do the same", a threat to *you*, and nothing about the tiny people you just switched off. For a class this is the most interesting fork in the vignette (do simulated minds count morally? cf. Chalmers, *Reality+*, already on the to-do list), and it goes by without comment.
  Evidence: `off_end`, card text "Every little world went dark at once..." (t=196.1 s); shot 046-t193.jpg.
  Suggestion: (a) give the switch-off reflection one moral beat before the prudential one, e.g. "They were real to themselves. Were they real to you?" (b) Make the credence question playable. Before the reveal, let the player count: "Run more worlds" could add shelves until the counter reads "1 world like yours that isn't inside a dome · 1,000 that are". Then the pull-back makes the odds land. (c) Name the trilemma's three horns in the notebook as three things the scene *showed*: worlds that never got built, a builder who chooses not to run them (switch-off), worlds that are mostly nested.
- **[minor] [decision design]** Both endings arrive in one choice, with no rewind or replay acknowledgement, and there's no "doing nothing" option: after the reveal you must either switch off or walk out of the door. For this experiment, *not deciding* ("I'll leave it and keep working") is the stance most people actually take.
  Suggestion: if the player stands still during `choose` for ~20 s, add a third ending: the narrator notes that nothing changes whether you know or not, which is the "it makes no practical difference" response (and it ties straight into the journal question).
- **[minor] [collision]** After "Look out of the window" the player ends up behind the desk at (1.1, -4.6) (`walkable` = false). From there, `use run more worlds` couldn't reach the prompt within 20 s. I had to tap-walk out to (1.5, 1). A student would think the lever was broken.
  Evidence: t≈93–145 s, shot 039-t106.jpg.
  Suggestion: put the window's interact point in front of the desk, or clamp the scripted approach to walkable ground.
- **[keep]** The pull-back reveal (shot 052-t235.jpg: your study under glass, a giant face peering in) is the single best "aha" image in the hall. It does in four seconds what a lecture on nested simulation takes ten minutes to do. The window line ("You've never actually checked how far it goes") and the unremembered photo are perfect Cartesian asides. The journal question ("would anything you care about change?") is a great classroom prompt.

### fermi-paradox
- **[major] [philosophy/decision design]** The only choice (send a message / keep listening) belongs to a *different* debate, whether we should broadcast (METI/"active SETI"), and it doesn't let the player do anything with the actual paradox. The setup is excellent and faithful to Hart: "Billions of stars... Some are far older than the Sun. Someone should have come by now. So where is everybody?" But the player never gets to act on an *answer* to "where is everybody?". The three classic families (rare, quiet or self-destroyed, early) are only read out in the listen ending ("Either they're rare, or they're quiet, or we're early"). The notebook and journal to-dos are all about Fermi/Hart and have nothing on the send-or-not debate that the journal question ("Would you send the message...?") actually asks about. So the student is asked to justify a choice the reading doesn't cover.
  Evidence: `listen_end` at t=382.2 s; journal question on both cards; notebook text (t≈357 s).
  Suggestion: either (a) add the METI side to the notebook and to-dos (e.g. David Brin, "The 'Great Silence'", 1983; the 2015 open statement against METI; the Arecibo message 1974, a real message that is "still travelling"), or better (b) make "Keep listening" playable as the paradox. Each night you listen, the lapse can show one hypothesis as an image: a star whose planet goes dark (self-destruction), a dish somewhere else pointed at *you*, also silent (everyone listening, no one sending, which ties the two debates together), an empty sky with a single light far away (we're early). Then the choice to send lands *after* the student has seen the reasons for silence.
- **[minor] [pacing]** Both endings come within ~15 s of the single action, with no rewind or "the choice comes round again" beat. The vignette is ~90 s long if you skip the asides, well under the 3–8 minutes in GAME.md, and the second run is identical up to the button.
  Suggestion: after the first ending, replay could open with "The logbook has one new line in it: yours." and the narrator acknowledging the second choice, as GAME.md's replay rule asks.
- **[keep]** "Someone should have come by now" is the cleanest plain-language statement of Hart's argument I know. The moon line ("Only one other world has ever had visitors. They left footprints, and went home.") is a beautiful reversal: *we* are the only aliens we know of. The logbook with "the same entry. Nothing." makes the silence something you can read. And the journal question works for any student, whichever ending they got.

### tragedy-of-the-commons
- **[major] [decision design]** The dilemma has no pull because your private gain is invisible. "One more sheep. A little more for you." is said, but nothing on screen shows it: no wool, coin, fatter sheep, or "your share" counter. The only visible effect of adding is the shared grass going down. Pressing "Add a sheep" is then simply the wrong button, and any student will ring the bell. A commons dilemma teaches only when the individual incentive is felt *and* the collective cost is felt.
  Evidence: runs 1–3; `add_1` at t=491.2 s; shots 065-t495.jpg, 068-t557.jpg.
  Suggestion: give the player a visible, private reward per sheep: a wool bale that stacks at your pen, or a small tally by your house that keeps ticking up while the grass lasts. It should visibly out-earn your neighbours for a while. Then the question "So why would anyone stop?" has a real answer on screen.
- **[major] [philosophy]** The neighbours never overgraze on their own. They only copy you (`for o in 1..4 addSheep` inside *your* onUse), and with no adds the pasture is in perfect equilibrium forever (I waited 60 s: grass stayed at 1.0). So the game teaches "the tragedy happens because one bad actor starts it", which is the opposite of Lloyd and Hardin: in their model each herder *independently* reasons to add, and no villain is needed. The card's "Every sheep made sense to the one who added it" is then contradicted by the neighbour's line "Well, you added one. Why shouldn't I?".
  Suggestion: have a neighbour add a sheep on their own after ~30 s of the player doing nothing. The player then faces the real choice: match them, hold back alone, or call a meeting. The neighbours' copying can stay as a second reason (reciprocity), not the only one.
- **[major] [decision design]** You can't *remove* a sheep, so unilateral restraint, the most instructive option, doesn't exist. "So why would anyone stop?" is asked after you've already added and the decline is locked in (after one add, the grass collapses in ~95 s whatever you do, unless you ring). The classroom lesson is that if you take yours back alone, the others keep theirs, so restraint alone doesn't save the commons.
  Suggestion: add "Take a sheep back" at your pen. The neighbours don't follow ("More for us, then."), the decline slows but doesn't stop, and the reflection names it: "You held back. It wasn't enough on your own."
- **[minor] [philosophy/idea]** The notebook names three answers (privatise, regulate from above, Ostrom's self-governance), but only Ostrom is playable (the bell). That's a missed chance to map the reading onto acts.
  Suggestion: add a "Fence off your share" option (the pasture splits into six small fenced plots; yours is fine, the thin ones next door struggle; reflection: "It worked. Nobody shares anything any more."). Also put Hardin's own phrase, "mutual coercion, mutually agreed upon", in the notebook next to the bell ending so students can see Hardin and Ostrom disagree less than the summary suggests.
- **[minor] [bug/narrative]** Ringing the bell straight away (3 s in, no sheep added) plays the intro line "Five neighbours, two sheep each. The grass is thick." *between* "You ring the bell" and "Together, you agree on limits". The card then says "The grass came back", but it never left. The same card and text show whether you rang pre-emptively or after overgrazing to 0.09.
  Evidence: run 2, t=606.7–623.9 s; run 3 recoverFrom 0.09.
  Suggestion: cancel the intro coroutine when the phase leaves `graze`. Use a separate card for the pre-emptive bell ("You agreed on limits before anyone needed them. That is rarer than it sounds.") and make the late bell cost something: the recovery takes a season, or one neighbour holds out.
- **[minor] [decision design]** The meeting is instant and unanimous, including the neighbour who had just said "Not my fault. I only did what everyone did." The bell is a free win button, so the hard part of Ostrom (monitoring, sanctions, trust) is skipped.
  Suggestion: make the agreement a small act: after the meeting, you walk your own extra sheep out first (a credible commitment), and one neighbour follows only after you do.
- **[keep]** The neighbours' lines getting sourer as the grass thins ("Well, you added one. Why shouldn't I?", "Not my fault. I only did what everyone did.") are the best dialogue in the hall. They voice the free-rider logic in plain words. The journal question ("something shared in your own life... what keeps it from being used up?") is the best transfer question in the set.

## Keep
- Journal questions for all five: open, "what and why", work for any ending, and transfer to students' lives (the commons one especially).
- The simulation pull-back reveal (your study under a dome, a giant face peering in).
- Plain-language statements of the arguments: "Someone should have come by now"; "You could have stopped him, in a sense. You just didn't."; "Every sheep made sense to the one who added it."
- The asides that quietly deepen the idea: the moon footprints (Fermi), the unremembered photo and the unchecked sky (Simulation), the neighbours' souring lines (Commons), the all-q page (Monkeys).
- The notebooks' summaries are accurate and name the rival positions. They're ready to be turned into playable options.

## Harness notes
- `use run more worlds` failed ("could not get the prompt within 20s") because the player was standing on non-walkable ground behind the desk after a previous `use`. A manual `walk` fixed it. That may be a game collision issue as much as a harness one (see the simulation-argument finding).
