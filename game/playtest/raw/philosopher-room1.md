# philosopher · room 1 (house, trolley-problem, brain-in-a-vat, platos-cave, ship-of-theseus)

## Summary
- The scenarios are chosen and staged well, and the notebooks are accurate (I checked every citation I could: Foot 1967, Thomson 1976/1985/2008, Putnam 1981, *Republic* 514a–521b, Plutarch *Thes.* 23, Hobbes *De Corpore* II.11 are all right). The problems are in the **narration and ending cards**, which keep taking sides the notebooks don't.
- **Leading endings** are the biggest pattern: the Theseus endings affirm the repaired ship and question the old one; the trolley's final line ("You only decide who") says doing nothing is choosing victims, which is the point in dispute; the trolley self-sacrifice card ("The trolley only needed someone on the track") blurs the switch/footbridge distinction the whole literature rests on.
- **Two vignettes drift from their source**: Brain in a Vat makes the deception detectable (a flickering edge you can step off) and never touches Putnam, which is its reason to exist apart from Descartes; Plato's Cave drops the compelled, painful ascent and the duty to go back down, both of which the game's own notebook describes.
- The narration gives the do-nothing trolley player two challenges but the lever-puller only one, and only if they pull on the first run.
- The trolley's third track (Thomson 2008), the Cave's pond (the reflections stage), the fisherman's grandad's rod (the grandfather's axe) and the shipwright are really good plain-language philosophy. The fixes are mostly a line or two each.

## Findings

### house (journal and portals)
- **[minor] [journal] [philosophy]** The journal keeps one answer per vignette but shows only the *latest* ending, so an answer written for one ending ends up labelled with another. My Cave answer ("I was obliged to go back down") now sits under "You stayed in the light". For a journal meant to record *what you did and why*, that misattributes the player's reasoning.
  Evidence: journal at t=1039s after three Cave endings.
  Suggestion: store the answer with the ending it was written after (or list each ending reached with its own answer).
- **[minor] [attribution]** Trolley to-do: "Judith Jarvis Thomson, 'The Trolley Problem' (1985), who turned it into the puzzle". Thomson named it and moved it to the bystander at the switch in 1976 ("Killing, Letting Die, and the Trolley Problem", *The Monist*), which the notebook correctly cites and the journal skips.
  Suggestion: "then Judith Jarvis Thomson, who named it in 1976 and put a bystander at the switch ('The Trolley Problem', 1985)…"
- **[minor] [philosophy]** Brain-in-a-vat to-do: Putnam "argues you can't even coherently say it". His claim is that "I am a brain in a vat" is *self-refuting* (if a vat-brain says it, it comes out false, since its "brain" and "vat" don't refer to real brains and vats), not that it's incoherent or unsayable.
  Suggestion: "He argues that if you were one, the sentence 'I am a brain in a vat' would come out false."

### brain-in-a-vat
- **[major] [philosophy]** The skeptical scenario is made *detectable*. The world has a visible edge that "flickers", and you can step off it into the lab. The whole force of the brain-in-a-vat (and the evil demon) is that the deception is experientially *indistinguishable* from reality. Here the player is asked "If none of this were real, how would you ever know?" while standing at the answer (the glitching edge). It plays like a red-pill story, not the skeptic's argument.
  Evidence: t=48–53s, (9,15): captions "The world ends here. Just past the edge, it flickers." then "If none of this were real, how would you ever know?"; shots/003-edge.jpg (the edge bands are visible).
  Suggestion: make the edge seamless in layer 0 (no flicker, nothing to see) and have the step-off be an act of *pure doubt* (e.g. walking out past where the world plainly continues; the ground just keeps going until you choose to "stop believing it"). Or keep the flicker but let the narrator own the move: "In the real version, there'd be no edge to find."
- **[major] [philosophy]** Putnam's reply, which is what makes "Brain in a Vat" (as opposed to Descartes' demon) its own thought experiment, is absent from play and narration. The notebook and journal both headline it ("He argues you can't even coherently say it"), but nothing in the vignette touches reference or meaning; the only lab line that brushes it is "Somewhere in here is that tree." What the player enacts is generic Cartesian skepticism plus an infinite regress.
  Evidence: game/lines/brain-in-a-vat.json (all 16 lines); layer 1 "Look at the machine" at t=83s.
  Suggestion: use the machine or the window for one quiet externalist beat, e.g. at the machine in layer 1: "When you said 'tree' up there, did you mean this? Or the tree you've never touched?" That is plain-language Putnam with no names.
- **[minor] [philosophy]** The nested labs (three layers, "No way out") turn the scenario into a regress/simulation-stack, which is closer to the Simulation Argument in the hall than to the vat. There's a risk players will think brain-in-a-vat *is* the idea of worlds inside worlds.
  Evidence: layer 2 at t=118s, "Another brain. Another machine. Another world above it."; shots/010-t126.jpg.
  Suggestion: keep the regress but have the ending card name the actual point (you can't get an outside check), which it half does already ("You can only ever check the world with the world."), and cut the visual echo of the simulation dome in the hall, or add a notebook line distinguishing the two.
- **[minor] [philosophy]** Embodiment is inconsistent: the premise is that *you* are the brain, yet after stepping off you walk around the lab as the teal figure, looking at the brain from outside, while a copy of you sits on the bench above. It implies you are something other than the brain (a soul that can leave?), which undercuts the setup.
  Evidence: shots/005-t67.jpg and 007-t73.jpg (teal figure in lab, small figure on bench in the world above).
  Suggestion: make the lab camera the view *from* the vat (no teal body in the lab), or have the narrator acknowledge it: "You're standing here. So who's in the jar?"
- **[minor] [philosophy]** The journal question ("If you could never, ever tell the difference, would it matter to you whether your world was real?") is the Experience Machine's question (value), not the vat's (knowledge / meaning). Both endings also lean evaluative ("Real or not, it's the only sun you have").
  Evidence: game-over card at t=164s; shots/011-t167.jpg.
  Suggestion: ask the epistemic question, e.g. "Can you know right now that you're not a brain in a vat? If you can't, do you know you have hands?" Or keep this one and mention the Experience Machine in the notebook's related-ideas.
- **[minor] [clarity]** The "You stayed" ending fires the moment you sit, even if you never saw the edge or heard the central question. On replay I sat at t=6s and got "You never found out whether the sun was real" having never been told there was anything to find out.
  Evidence: second run, t=175–189s.
  Suggestion: disable "Sit down" until `edgeSeen`, or have sitting before the question play a different, shorter line and not end the vignette.
- **[polish] [notebook]** The notebook opens with "This short film traces the idea from René Descartes' evil demon (1641) to Hilary Putnam's…" In the game there is no short film on screen, so "this short film" is confusing. It also omits Gilbert Harman's *Thought* (1973), usually cited as the first explicit brain-in-a-vat formulation, before Putnam.
  Evidence: `N` at t=16s.
  Suggestion: rewrite the intro for the game ("This scene traces…", link to the film separately) and add Harman 1973 to "Where it comes from".
- **[polish] [portal]** The portal is "the book on the lectern" (Descartes' *Meditations*, per GAME.md). Fine as a precursor, but the demon is not a vat and Descartes offers an answer (God is no deceiver) that the vignette doesn't touch. The label "Brain in a Vat" on a 1641 book is a small anachronism a pedant will notice.
  Suggestion: make the lectern book visibly two books, or a 1981 paperback lying on top of the old one.

### trolley-problem
- **[major] [philosophy]** The self-sacrifice ending card says "The trolley only needed someone on the track." That is false of the switch case and it smuggles in the exact confusion the trolley literature exists to separate: in the switch case the one on the side track is *not needed* (if he walked off, the five would still be saved); his death is a foreseen side effect. Being *needed* (used as a means) is what marks the footbridge and loop cases. The line quietly tells players the switch and footbridge are the same.
  Evidence: game-over card "You chose yourself" at t=468s.
  Suggestion: "The five walk away. So does the one. This time the only one in the way was you." or "Someone had to be in the way. You made sure it was you."
- **[major] [philosophy] [narration]** The closing line and card take a side on the doing/allowing question while claiming to be neutral: "Whatever you choose, someone is standing on the track. You only decide who." and "Nobody could stop the trolley. You only decided where it went." On a doing/allowing view (Foot 1967, and Thomson 2008 by the notebook's own account) leaving the lever alone is *not* deciding who dies in the same sense; it is letting the five die rather than killing the one. The line grades the do-nothing player as a chooser of victims, contrary to GAME.md §5 ("Never tell the player they were right or wrong").
  Evidence: three-run ending at t=353–360s (runs: nothing, pull, nothing).
  Suggestion: keep the observation but let it cut both ways: "Every time, someone was on the track. Whether leaving it alone counts as choosing is the part nobody agrees on." For the card: "You ran it 3 times. The trolley never stopped. Only you can say whether standing still was a choice."
- **[major] [narration]** The cost of pulling is only ever said if you pull on your first run. Line `pulled_2` ("But the one on the side track was never in danger, until you moved the trolley.") is replaced by `again_diff` on later runs. My first playthrough went nothing → pull → nothing, and I never heard any reflection on pulling except "The five are safe." So the utilitarian choice went unchallenged while the do-nothing choice was challenged twice ("you could have changed it", "You only decide who").
  Evidence: run 2 at t=307–311s: "You pulled the lever. The five are safe." then "This time you chose the other way. Most people feel the pull of both."; compare run 1 of the second playthrough at t=410–414s where `pulled_2` plays.
  Suggestion: on a changed choice, play `pulled_1`/`stayed_1` + the matching `_2` line the first time each branch is taken, then `again_diff` (or fold "This time you chose the other way" into the first line).
- **[minor] [philosophy] [variants]** The journal question is about the footbridge ("wouldn't push someone off a bridge to stop the same trolley"), a case the player never sees. The footbridge (and Thomson's loop) are the variants that make the trolley problem a problem; a switch-only vignette mostly shows the easy half.
  Evidence: game-over card, t=360s.
  Suggestion: add a footbridge over the main line on replay (a heavy-set worker leaning on the rail, with a push prompt) or a loop track variant, instead of or alongside the third track. If not, ask a question about what was played (e.g. the third-track question the narrator already poses).
- **[minor] [philosophy] [notebook]** The third track is Thomson's 2008 "three options" argument (if you wouldn't turn it onto yourself, you may not turn it onto the one), but nothing in the notebook ties the twist to that paper. The narrator's "If not, is it fair to send it to someone else?" is a faithful plain-language version of it, which is great; the notebook should say so.
  Evidence: twist line at t=267s; notebook text (`N`, t=364s) only says "ends with Thomson's surprising change of mind in 2008".
  Suggestion: add one sentence to the notebook: "The third track is Thomson's own 2008 test: the bystander can also turn the trolley onto himself." Also replace "This short film walks through…" (there is no film in the game).
- **[minor] [narration] [pacing]** If you walk straight to the lever (the obvious thing to do), you pull it before the stakes are narrated: the lever line plays, then "Five people…", "On the side track, just one." and "Should you pull it?" arrive after the lever is already pulled. On the first playthrough "Should you pull it?" also came *before* "This lever switches the tracks." The choice is made uninformed, and the central question is asked after it's answered.
  Evidence: second playthrough t=369–383s (pulled at 369.8s, "Should you pull it?" at 383.3s); first playthrough t=226.5s vs 228.3s.
  Suggestion: hold the lever's prompt until "Should you pull it?" has played (or play `five`/`one` on arrival regardless of position), and drop "Should you pull it?" if the lever is already pulled.
- **[minor] [consistency]** The player can stand on the main track among the five; the trolley passes through the player, who stays upright while the five fly. Given that the game's own twist is "the trolley comes for you instead", having your body ignored on the main line is inconsistent (and invites the "just stand in front of it" objection the game can't answer).
  Evidence: t=244–247s at (12.2, 0.3); shots/014-t247.jpg.
  Suggestion: keep the player off the rails (walkable mask), or have the workers shoo you off gently.
- **[polish] [philosophy]** "Most people feel the pull of both" is fine, but "Most people would pull the lever" in the journal question is an empirical claim; worth a notebook footnote (Greene et al. 2001; Hauser et al. 2007 found ~85–90% for switch) so it isn't just asserted.
  Suggestion: add the survey citation to the notebook.

### ship-of-theseus
- **[major] [philosophy] [narration]** The two endings are asymmetric in a way that endorses one answer. Boarding the repaired ship gets a verdict stated as fact: "Its parts changed. Its story didn't." and the card "Every plank was replaced, but it never stopped being the ship that sailed." Boarding the old-plank ship gets a doubt: "Every plank is the same. But is the ship?" and "Whether that makes it the same ship is the question." The game sides with continuity over constitution, and questions only the player who chose the matter. This breaks GAME.md §5 ("never tell the player they were right or wrong") and misrepresents a live debate. (Also, factually, the repaired ship never sailed in this scene; it sat at the dock the whole time.)
  Evidence: new-ship ending t=608–615s; old-ship ending t=804–811s.
  Suggestion: give both endings the same shape (a plain description, then the question turned back): new: "You chose the ship that kept going. Not one plank of it was there at the start. Is that enough?"; old: "You chose the original wood. It was all there at the start. But was it a ship, all that time in a pile?" Cards: "…it never stopped being in use. Whether that makes it the same ship is the question." / "…every original plank. Whether that makes it the same ship is the question."
- **[minor] [philosophy] [variants]** Only two options. The literature's main moves beyond "the repaired one" vs "the old wood" are missing: *both* (two ships coinciding / it's indeterminate), *neither* (identity doesn't survive such change: mereological essentialism), and the "it depends what you mean by 'same'" deflation (Hobbes's own answer turned on whether identity follows matter, form or the aggregate of accidents). There's also no way to do nothing: once the old ship is built you must board one to finish, which breaks the pillar "doing nothing is always a real option".
  Evidence: interactables in `choose` phase are only the two "Board…" prompts; waiting 20s at t=782–803s does nothing.
  Suggestion: let the player walk back up the dock without boarding: after a long silence, "You didn't pick. Maybe 'the same ship' was never one question." → a third card ("You left them both"). This also gives the vignette its doing-nothing branch.
- **[minor] [philosophy] [visual]** "Not one original plank is left" is true, but the repaired ship keeps its original keel, mast and sail (only six side planks are swapped), and the rebuilt ship appears with its own keel, mast and sail from nowhere. A pedant will say the repaired ship obviously wins on the keel alone, and ask where the second mast came from.
  Evidence: shots/025-t607.jpg (both ships fully rigged; the replaced ship's dark keel and mast never change).
  Suggestion: make the mast/keel part of the replacement (a 7th and 8th swap), or have the rebuilt hull come up bare, with the shipwright stepping the *old* mast that was replaced last.
- **[minor] [narration]** "The ship of Theseus." names the experiment in the voice. It's a mythic name, not a philosopher, so it's within the letter of GAME.md §5, but it's the only vignette I played whose narration names its own title (the trolley just says "A runaway trolley.").
  Suggestion: "A very old ship. Its wood is getting old." or "The hero's ship, kept in the harbour." and let the portal label carry the name.
- **[polish] [philosophy]** Replay doesn't acknowledge a different choice (GAME.md: "The narrator acknowledges whether you chose the same or differently"). For this puzzle the second pass is where the tension is: the player who sailed each has just endorsed two incompatible criteria.
  Evidence: second run (old wood) got exactly the same lines as a first run.
  Suggestion: on a changed choice: "Last time you went with the ship. This time, the wood. They can't both be the one."

### platos-cave
- **[major] [philosophy]** The release and ascent lose the element Plato insists on: compulsion and pain. In *Republic* 515c–516a the prisoner is *released and compelled* to stand and turn, it hurts, he'd rather go back to the shadows, and he is *dragged* up the steep ascent against his will. Here the chains simply "come loose", nobody makes you do anything, and you stroll to the mouth in a few seconds. That changes the allegory from "education is a painful turning of the whole soul, done to you by someone who already knows" (518c–d) into "curious individuals find the truth on their own". The notebook itself says he "is dragged up into the sunlight", so the game contradicts its own notebook.
  Evidence: t=846s "Your chains have come loose."; mouth reached t=911s with no resistance; `game/notebook/platos-cave.json` summary.
  Suggestion: have a figure (a released carrier, or a hand from the dark) pull you to your feet and walk you towards the fire; make the first steps towards the mouth resisted (slowed, the light pushing you back) with one line: "Someone pulls you up. It hurts to look." Keep "Sit back down" as the refusal Plato predicts ("he would turn away and flee back to the things he could see").
- **[major] [philosophy]** "Stay in the light" and "go back and tell them" are presented as two neutral personal preferences. For Plato the whole point of the ending is that the one who has seen the sun *must* go back down (519c–520c: "we won't allow them to stay up there"), and that going back is dangerous (517a: they would kill him if they could). The notebook mentions "the philosopher's duty to go back down" but no line in play does. Result: the political half of the allegory is missing, and the stay ending reads as a legitimate "happy" end.
  Evidence: "You could stay up here. Or go back down, and tell them." (t=919s); cards "You stayed in the light / The others are still down there, watching the wall." and "You went back / …They would rather keep the shadows."
  Suggestion: add one reflective line to the stay ending that names the pull without grading: "The others are still down there. You're the only one who knows the way." And in the tell ending, let the prisoners' hostility go one notch further than laughter ("Don't bring that up here again." / they turn their backs), and note 517a (the threat of death, read as Socrates' fate) in the notebook.
- **[minor] [bug] [narration]** Choosing to stay before the "choose" line has played causes that line to play *after* the ending: I sat under the tree at t=1030.8s ("You stay in the light."), and at t=1032.9s the narrator said "You could stay up here. Or go back down, and tell them." over the game-over card. It reads as the narrator second-guessing the player.
  Evidence: second replay, t=1030–1035s; shots/041-t1035.jpg.
  Suggestion: cancel the queued `choose` line when an ending fires (or disable "Sit under the tree" until `S.saidChoose`).
- **[minor] [philosophy] [pacing]** The ascent skips Plato's stages. Outside, you immediately get "The real things, and the sun that lights them all." In 516a–b the freed prisoner can first only look at shadows, then reflections in water, then the things themselves, then the night sky, and only last the sun. The pond ("Your reflection. Another kind of shadow, but this one looks back.") is a lovely nod to the reflection stage, but it's optional and comes *after* you've been told you're seeing real things and the sun.
  Evidence: t=913–923s.
  Suggestion: make the first seconds outside washed out (as it already is at the mouth), let the pond be the first thing you can see clearly, and hold the "sun that lights them all" line until the player has looked at the pond or a tree. That's the ascent in miniature.
- **[minor] [clarity]** "Sit back down" is the only prompt at the moment the chains fall, and it's where you're standing. A player who presses E to see what happens ends the vignette 0.3 s after being freed, and hears "You turn back to the wall" although they never turned. Philosophically the refusal should come *after* the pain of seeing the fire (515d–e), not before.
  Evidence: replay t=984.5–984.8s.
  Suggestion: enable "Sit back down" only after the player has turned to face the fire (`voice.said.has('turn')`), and change the line to "You look away from the fire, back to the wall. It's easier."
- **[minor] [journal]** The journal question ("Think of something you once believed completely and later saw differently…") presumes you turned around and came out; for the "You kept watching" ending it doesn't fit. It also reframes the Cave as a personal-conversion story; Plato frames it as an image of "our nature in its education and want of education" (514a).
  Suggestion: keep the question but vary the lead-in by ending, or ask about education: "Who turned you around, the last time you really changed your mind? Could you have done it alone?"
- **[polish] [notebook]** It's an allegory, not a thought experiment in the modern sense; the notebook could say so in one line and note it's the third of three linked images (Sun, Line, Cave), since the sun here stands for the Good. Also the gutenberg "Jowett" link and 514a–521b are right; good.
- **[polish] [replay]** No acknowledgement of a different choice on replay (GAME.md template), in any of my three runs.

## Keep
- The trolley's third track and "Would you? If not, is it fair to send it to someone else?": Thomson's 2008 argument in two plain sentences.
- The fainted driver ("He'll wake up later with no idea any of this happened."): it quietly makes it Thomson's bystander case, not Foot's driver.
- `pulled_2`: "the one on the side track was never in danger, until you moved the trolley". The best doing/allowing line in the game; it should play for everyone who pulls.
- Cave prisoners' chatter ("I've counted. The bird always comes after the jar.", "What's behind us? Nothing. Just rock.") is 516c's honours for predicting the shadows, done perfectly; the pond reflection is the reflections stage.
- Theseus: the fisherman's grandad's rod (the grandfather's axe) and the shipwright's "Seemed a shame to burn them" (Hobbes's reassembler).
- Brain in a vat: "You can only ever check the world with the world." is a sharp, fair statement of the skeptic's point.
- Notebooks: accurate, well chosen and properly separated from the voice.
